# Research and testing with neurodivergent participants

Guidelines prevent obvious harm. They don't tell you whether *your* product works. Only testing does.

## Recruiting

- Recruit neurodivergent participants **into your normal studies**, not only into a separate "accessibility study". Segregated research produces segregated fixes.
- Say what accommodations are available in the screener, before asking anyone to disclose anything.
- Never require a diagnosis. Most neurodivergent adults are undiagnosed — diagnosis is gated by money, gender, and race. Recruit on self-identification and on task difficulty ("Do you find forms with numbers difficult?").
- Pay properly and pay promptly. Neurodivergent people are over-represented in unemployment and under-employment; unpaid research extracts from the people it claims to serve.
- Include people who have given up on your product. They're the finding.

## Before the session

Send, at least 48 hours ahead:

- What will happen, in order, with times.
- Who will be there, their names, and their roles.
- Whether cameras are needed (default: no).
- The questions you'll ask, if that's not the thing being tested.
- How long it will take, and that breaks are fine.
- How to stop, and that stopping costs them nothing.
- A photo of the room or a link to the exact meeting URL.

Ask what would make it easier: time of day, session length, written vs verbal, camera on/off, breaks, a support person present, questions in advance, a quiet room, no bright lights.

## During

- Say up front: "We're testing the design, not you. If something is confusing, that's our fault."
- Allow silence. Processing time is not confusion — do not fill the gap.
- Don't demand think-aloud. Verbalising while doing is a dual task that penalises exactly the people you recruited. Offer retrospective walkthrough instead.
- Ask one question at a time. Concrete, not hypothetical. "What would you do next?" beats "How do you imagine you'd feel about…"
- Avoid: rapid-fire follow-ups, forced eye contact, tight timeboxes, surprise tasks, "just talk me through it" pressure.
- Watch for masking. Politeness and "no, it's fine, I'm just being slow" often cover a real failure. Note where they hesitated, not only what they said.
- Offer breaks at natural boundaries; take them even if the participant says they're fine.
- Keep sessions shorter than you would otherwise — 45 minutes, not 90.

## Analysis

- Weight behaviour over self-report. People apologise for interfaces that failed them.
- Hesitation, re-reading, backtracking, and abandonment are findings even when the task technically completed.
- Look for where working memory was the bottleneck: did they scroll back? write something down? ask you to repeat?
- Don't aggregate neurodivergent participants into a single "accessibility" bucket in the report. The needs conflict; the report should show that.

## Ethics

- Never disclose a participant's neurotype outside the research team, including in the report.
- Don't record sensitive disclosures you don't need.
- If someone becomes distressed, stop. Pay them anyway.
- Don't use research to diagnose, and don't relay speculation about a participant.

## Making your own team accessible

Neurodivergent researchers and designers exist and are frequently the reason a product got fixed. Support that, don't only study it:

- Agendas in advance; notes and recordings after.
- Written channels equal to verbal ones — decisions in a meeting nobody logged excludes people.
- No surprise "quick calls".
- Meeting-free blocks for deep work.
- Cameras optional, always.
- Sensory considerations in the office: lighting, noise, scent, quiet space (the BBC Sensory Environment Checklist is a good audit tool for this).
- Ask what helps; don't assume the accommodation.
